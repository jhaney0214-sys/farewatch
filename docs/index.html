Farewatch

A deal tracker that ranks on what a trip would actually cost you, not on the number in the headline. The fare is the smaller half of the truth, and no deal feed anywhere prices the other half.

The deal newsletters broadcast. They find a $340 fare to Lisbon and mail it to two million people, most of whom cannot fly those dates, do not live near that airport, and are not interested in Lisbon. Personalisation is the thing they charge for, and even the paid tiers are shallow.

Farewatch inverts that. It pulls the same public feeds — flights, hotels, packages, cruises — works out what each deal would cost once currency and on-the-ground prices are counted, and ranks what survives against constraints you actually have. Every score carries its reasons with it.

The fare is the smaller half

A $300 flight to Zurich and a $450 flight to Bangkok are not a $150 difference. Currency is the third piece, and it moves exactly the half that fares do not. A fare is already quoted in your own money; food, hotels and taxis are not. A country whose currency has slid 15% against yours is 15% off before any deal is applied. Farewatch compares today's rate against the trailing year and folds the difference into the trip cost.

What a score is made of

Eight components, each bounded and each returning its own explanation, so a ranking is auditable rather than a number to take on faith.

A deal leaving from an airport you cannot reach is not discarded. It is priced — the hop to the deal's origin is costed and folded into the total, so an unreachable bargain competes on its honest number rather than on a fiction.

What validation found

The model was built first and checked against real market data afterwards, on 2026-08-29. Two of the three checks found it wrong, which is the only reason the check was worth running.

The parsing was right

Top-ranked deal, Asiana Los Angeles to Bangkok, quoted at $741 roundtrip. The cheapest fare on those exact dates was $742. Price, route, cabin, roundtrip flag and the seven-night assumption were all correct.

The value judgement was not

Farewatch called it 25% below typical. Google called prices "currently typical", and the same fare appeared on a February 2027 search — so it was the going rate, not a sale. The cause is structural: fares scale sub-linearly with distance and the model's curve is a straight line, running $0.129 per km on short haul against $0.068 on long haul, so it overestimates long routes badly.

The constants were not refitted. Seventeen noisy deal prices and one verified fare is not enough to replace one eyeballed number with another. Instead the claim now states its own provenance: est. 25% below typical from the curve, against cheaper than 15 of the 16 tracked to Bangkok from observation. One is a guess, one is a measurement, and they no longer look alike.

The model was overstating what it costs to sleep in rich countries while understating what it costs to get around cheap ones — and both biases flattered exactly the comparison the whole project is built on.

How it runs

No third-party packages, no API keys, no paid data. Feeds are cached, one bad source never stops a run, and an unreadable deal is dropped rather than guessed at. Observed prices are banked as ratios at five levels of specificity and correct the fare curve where the evidence supports it — so the model gets less wrong the longer it runs.
